From Access to Agency: Making Assistive Technology Work Beyond the Classroom

Devices open possibilities. Instruction, reliable access, and personal choice help turn those possibilities into everyday skills.

By Rob Harris · #AdvocateDad · November 15, 2026

All blog articles

A student receives a device. The school documents that assistive technology is available. Everyone moves to the next item on the agenda.

There is another conversation we need to have.

Can the student use it when the teacher is unavailable? Can they choose the right tool, recognize when it gives an unreliable answer, and ask for help on their own terms? Will they still have access to it after graduation?

Those questions bring us from access to agency.

For blind and low vision students, access means being able to obtain information and participate. Agency means having the knowledge, support, and authority to decide what happens next.

We should expect our education and transition systems to support both.

Access remains essential

Braille, screen readers, magnification, tactile graphics, accessible documents, and qualified instruction remain fundamental.

AI adds possibilities to that foundation. It does not relieve schools, employers, or public agencies of responsibility for making their materials and services accessible.

A student should have accessible course materials even if an app can attempt to describe a photograph of the worksheet. Otherwise, the student inherits the work of capturing the image, checking the description, and resolving whatever the tool misses.

The question is whether technology expands the student’s options—and whether the underlying barriers are being addressed.

What AI can contribute

AI tools can generate image descriptions, extract text, summarize information, and answer follow-up questions. For example, Be My AI allows users to submit a photograph and ask questions about its description. That can provide another way to explore information when the tool suits the task. Be My Eyes user guidance

Consider a hypothetical student reviewing a flyer for a community event. They might use a screen reader to read the accessible text, ask an AI tool about an illustration, and contact the organizer to clarify an accommodation.

The useful outcome is the student understanding the event and deciding whether to attend.

Technology should support that decision. The student remains in charge.

Judgment belongs in the instruction

An AI description can sound confident and still be wrong. Be My Eyes explicitly warns against using its AI service for critical health or safety decisions. Be My Eyes terms of service

That makes judgment part of learning to use the tool.

Students need opportunities to practice checking an answer, noticing missing information, protecting private information, and choosing another method. AI descriptions should supplement qualified orientation and mobility instruction and established travel techniques. They should not be treated as dependable obstacle detection or street-crossing guidance.

Choosing human assistance can also be an exercise of independence. Agency includes deciding when assistance is useful, who provides it, and what information to share.

Four questions that make the planning concrete

1. What meaningful activity will this support?

Start with something the student wants or needs to do: read a work schedule, participate in a science lesson, compare recreation options, or communicate an accommodation request.

Then identify the instruction and tools that support that activity.

Assistive technology is itself an area of the Expanded Core Curriculum. Its use can also connect with independent living, career education, self-determination, and other ECC areas. Planning should make those connections explicit. APH ConnectCenter’s ECC overview

2. Who will teach the skills—and support continued use?

A device demonstration is a beginning.

Ask who will provide instruction, how often it will occur, how progress will be assessed, and what support the student and family need between lessons. Include troubleshooting and opportunities to practice.

IDEA requires IEP teams to consider a child’s need for assistive technology devices and services. When necessary for a free appropriate public education, providing the device and necessary services is a school responsibility. That is an individualized determination, rather than an automatic entitlement to a particular AI product. U.S. Department of Education guidance

3. Where will the student practice?

A skill demonstrated in a quiet classroom may need further instruction before it works in a busy community setting.

Depending on the student’s goals, practice might involve reading a community notice, locating information on a workplace website, or requesting clarification when a digital form is inaccessible.

Ask the team to identify relevant settings and the support needed in each. School-purchased assistive technology must be available at home or in other settings when the IEP team determines that access is necessary for the student to receive FAPE. 34 CFR § 300.105

4. What keeps access working?

A useful plan should address subscriptions, compatible devices, connectivity, repairs, updates, and technical support.

It should also address alternatives. What happens when the battery dies, the connection fails, or the tool cannot complete the task?

For transition planning, ask who owns the equipment, what remains available after school exit, and what arrangements must be completed beforehand. A successful classroom setup can still leave a student facing an avoidable interruption later.

Measure what the student can do

“Device provided” tells us something about delivery. It tells us much less about the student’s experience.

Better questions include:

  • Can the student complete the activity with the support they choose?
  • Can they recognize when the tool is unsuitable or unreliable?
  • Can they explain what assistance they need?
  • Can they use another method when necessary?
  • Does the technology help them pursue something that matters to them?

The answers will differ across students. For someone with additional disabilities, success may involve supported decision-making, accessible communication, or directing another person’s assistance.

Those outcomes deserve recognition too.

Put the student’s choices at the center

Families and professionals bring valuable knowledge. The student’s preferences must shape the plan.

That includes preferences about cameras, AI services, privacy, human assistance, and when to put a device away.

Giving someone a tool creates an opportunity. Teaching the skills, sustaining access, and respecting their decisions helps them use that opportunity.

At your next IEP or transition meeting, choose one meaningful activity. Ask the team to document the instruction, tools, practice, responsibilities, and evidence of progress needed to support it.

That is a practical place to begin moving from access to agency.

Rob Harris is a Colorado-based parent advocate and the voice behind #AdvocateDad. His work focuses on blind and low vision education, family participation, and improving the systems families navigate.
